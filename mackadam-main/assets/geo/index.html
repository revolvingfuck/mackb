Four and a half billion years in the making of Prince Edward Island

4.6 Ga  ·  541 Ma  ·  Precambrian

Before the island, a wanderer.

Long before there was a coast, there was a fragment — the Avalon terrane, a slip of microcontinent drifting near the southern edge of Gondwana, half a world away from where Prince Edward Island now sits.

Its deep basement rocks still lie thousands of metres beneath PEI. None of them have ever seen the sky.

Hidden basement · never exposed
541  ·  359 Ma  ·  Early Paleozoic

Continents collide.
Mountains rise.

Avalonia crashes into Laurentia — ancestral North America — and the fragments fuse near the equator into Laurussia. Three orogenies in succession (Taconic, Salinic, Acadian) heave the Appalachians skyward across what is now eastern Canada.

In the late Devonian, strike-slip faults tear the crust open. Pull-apart basins yawn across the tropics. One of them — the Maritimes Basin — is about to become a cradle.

Mountains born · basin opening
359  ·  252 Ma  ·  Birth of the red cradle

An equatorial floodplain.

Deep inside the supercontinent Pangaea, hundreds of kilometres from any ocean, monsoon rains drain the eroding Appalachians into a vast, hot, subsiding lowland. Braided rivers wander across the plain.

Around 300 to 290 million years ago, layer upon layer of sediment settles out. Iron in the soil oxidises — rusts — and stains everything red. These are the Miminegash, Egmont Bay, and Kildare Capes formations: the rock that is PEI.

The red rocks form · ~300 Ma
252  ·  66 Ma  ·  Mesozoic

Pangaea splits apart.

The supercontinent fractures. A new ocean — the Atlantic — begins to open to the east, and the future Maritimes drift slowly northward on the trailing edge of North America.

Around 200 to 150 million years ago, the entire red sandstone stack tilts gently, all at once. No new rock is laid down on PEI. Instead, erosion patiently planes the surface flat.

Atlantic opens · redbeds tilt
66 Ma  ·  2.6 Ma  ·  Cenozoic dawn

The land becomes a coast.

As the Atlantic widens, the region settles into a passive continental margin. Slow Paleogene and Neogene erosion lowers everything to a near-featureless coastal plain — the quiet preamble to what comes next.

Passive margin · coastal plain forms
2.6 Ma  ·  5,000 ya  ·  Ice age

Ice carves.
The sea returns.

The Laurentide Ice Sheet rolls over the region again and again — kilometres thick, scouring the bedrock, smoothing the soft red sandstones.

Around 13,000 years ago the ice begins to melt. The land rebounds. Sea levels rise. For a time, what will become PEI is connected by dry land to the mainland — and then, between 6,000 and 5,000 years ago, the rising Atlantic floods the Northumberland Strait. An island is born.

Glaciers retreat · the strait floods
Today

A low red island, on the edge of the Atlantic.

The cliffs you stand on are tropical floodplain mud, dried and rusted and pressed for 300 million years. Every wave you hear is gnawing a few more millimetres back into a world that was once Pangaea.

~5,000 years as an island
The Red Cradle

From equator to estuary.

Prince Edward Island’s red rocks formed deep inside equatorial Pangaea, roughly 300 million years ago, on a hot tropical floodplain hundreds of kilometres from any sea.

The island itself — the shape on the map, the coastline, the Northumberland Strait — is only about 5,000 years old. A geological newborn carved out of an ancient red cradle.
