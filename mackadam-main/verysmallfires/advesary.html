The Unnatural Deployment of Our Natural Adversary

And How I Stopped Being a Punk and Started Being an Anarchist

by Robert Mack

Essay • Political Philosophy • Anarchism

I. The Question That Wouldn’t Die

I wanted to be an archaeologist when I was a child. Not in the academic, grant-writing, slow-brush-in-the-dirt sense. I wanted to be Indiana Jones. I wanted to descend into hidden temples and find the thing underneath — the artifact, the codex, the origin story that explained why the world felt wrong in ways nobody around me could name.

I was handed a shovel and told to dig in the approved direction. Paychecks. Rent. Products. Societal responsibilities presented not as choices but as gravity — inescapable, unquestionable, the nature of things.

I kept asking why. Why work? To make money. Why money? To pay rent, to eat. Why rent? To live, to be part of society. For what? The question looped and nobody closed it.

A skeptic would say: the loop isn’t a feature, it’s exhaustion. Adults who didn’t have good answers because they were tired and working and never stopped to look. That’s partly true. Most people propagate the system the way a language propagates — not by choosing it, not by designing it, but by speaking it because it’s the only language they were ever given. No villain required. No boardroom.

Exhaustion is real. It is also, in this system, a deliverable. The machine does not need to lock the door if everyone is too tired to try the handle.

But here is the point the essay will keep returning to: a system does not need to be consciously designed to function as if it were. The loop that swallowed my childhood questions was not a conspiracy. It was something more durable than conspiracy — a set of incentives, five thousand years in refinement, that selects against the question being asked and answered. The sophistication is not in the intent. It is in the outcome: generation after generation of people who work to eat and eat to work and never once ask who built the arrangement that makes that seem like the only option.

That is what I was looking for in those temples. The origin of the loop. The load-bearing lie.

I found it. It took thirty-five years.

II. Before the Machine: The Campfire, the Cave, the Commons

The paintings at Lascaux Cave are approximately 17,000 years old. No one owned them. No one sold them. No gallery represented the artists, no algorithm curated their distribution, no patron commissioned them for brand alignment. They were made because human beings are, at their irreducible core, meaning-making creatures — animals that must externalize their inner life or be destroyed by it.

Oral traditions, communal song, harvest ritual, the shared life of pre-enclosure village commons — these were not industries. They were not products. They were the living tissue of community. Art was not separate from life; it was how life understood itself. The campfire was not a venue. It was the original commons.

The commons — land, water, forest, shared labor — functioned as the material expression of that same principle. You did not need permission to exist on the earth you were born on. You did not need to convert your time into currency to access the ground beneath your feet. The reciprocal economy — I have this, you have that, our survival is intertwined — operated on relationship and trust. It required knowing each other. It required community, and community was not a product you could sell back to people. That is precisely why it had to go.

A skeptic will say: you’re romanticizing. Lascaux was 17,000 years ago. Those people had shorter lives, higher infant mortality, no antibiotics, constant exposure to violence and starvation. You wouldn’t want to live there. You want the feeling of integration without the material deprivation.

True. The argument is not go back. It is: notice what was lost, and ask whether it had to be lost. The deprivation of prehistoric life is not an argument for industrial alienation. It is an argument for asking whether those are the only two options on the table.

The answer is no, and the evidence is specific. Consider the integrated agricultural systems that sustained communities across East Asia for millennia — the backyard pond model, in which fish, crops, animals, and human waste cycled through each other in closed loops requiring near-zero external input. These systems fed communities with deep efficiency, near-zero waste, and no need for external markets. They did not disappear because they failed. They disappeared because they succeeded on terms that made them incompatible with the extraction of surplus by a ruling class. You cannot tax a community that produces entirely for itself. You cannot create dependency where none exists. The elimination of self-sufficient systems was not progress. It was policy.

The policy had authors. Landowners in parliament. Colonial administrators in the field. They were not agents of a hidden hand — they were men acting on visible interests, within a system that rewarded extraction and punished self-sufficiency. The East India Company did not need to be part of a centuries-long conspiracy to dismantle the agricultural commons of Bengal. It needed only to exist, to have power, and to act in its own interest. No conspiracy required. Only power, organized.

III. The First Lie: Debt Before Money

The standard economic mythology goes like this: primitive humans bartered goods directly, found it clumsy and inefficient, and eventually invented money as a more convenient medium of exchange. This story is false. It has been known to be false for decades within anthropology and economic history. It persists because it is useful.

David Graeber, in Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2011), documented the archaeological and anthropological record with exhaustive precision. What he found — and what the field has not successfully refuted, though it has contested the details — is that no barter economy of the kind described in introductory economics textbooks has ever been found anywhere. What precedes money, in every case examined, is credit. And what precedes credit is debt.

The first recorded financial instruments are Mesopotamian clay tablets from approximately 3000 BCE: obligations — grain owed to temple administrators, labor owed to landholders. Debt is older than coin. Obligation is older than exchange. Money was not invented to make trade easier. It was invented to standardize and manage debt — to convert the messy, human, relationship-based reality of obligation into something abstract, transferable, and enforceable by a third party with weapons.

Graeber’s work is not without critics. Some economic historians argue he overstates the absence of barter-like exchange in small-scale societies and understates gift economies that functioned through reciprocal obligation. The scholarly debate is ongoing. What is not seriously contested is the central inversion: the barter-first story, told in every introductory economics textbook, is a myth economists invented, largely in the eighteenth century, to justify a theory of markets they wanted to be universal. The actual historical sequence — obligation first, money second — does not support that theory, which is why the myth persists.

There is a further complication worth acknowledging: Mesopotamian debt was not always inescapable. Jubilee traditions — periodic debt cancellations decreed by rulers — existed within these early systems. The shift from debt-that-can-be-forgiven to debt-that-is-permanent, from community-based credit where default meant shame to institution-based credit where default meant dispossession, is not a single moment. It is a long process. The pivot is not when debt appeared. It is when debt became structurally inescapable — when the alternatives to participation were systematically eliminated.

That elimination has a name and a history. The enclosure of the commons in medieval and early modern England — the conversion of shared land into private property, driving subsistence farmers from land their families had worked for generations into cities and factories — was not an economic evolution. It was organized dispossession, conducted through legal mechanisms written by the dispossessors.

Enclosure took land that had sustained families for generations — land held in common, worked collectively, passed down not through deeds but through use — and converted it into the exclusive property of whoever could document ownership in the new legal language. The farmers became trespassers on ground their own grandparents had worked. Then they became labor.

The Mesopotamian temple and the English enclosure act are separated by four thousand years and vastly different contexts. But the logic is identical: convert something people hold in common into something owned by a few, enforce that conversion through authority, and call the resulting dependency natural. The load-bearing lie is this: that the arrangement in which you must convert your time and labor into currency, in order to pay another human being for access to the earth you were born on, is natural. It is not natural. It is approximately five thousand years old — a rounding error in the 300,000-year story of Homo sapiens. It was built. It can be named.

IV. The Industrial Perfection of the Dependency Loop

The Industrial Revolution did not invent exploitation. It industrialized it — made it scalable, efficient, and self-reinforcing in ways that previous systems could not achieve. The specific transformation is this: it moved the site of production from the land and the home into the factory, and in doing so severed the last remaining connection most people had to direct subsistence.

Before industrialization, a dispossessed peasant was still a person with embodied skills — how to grow food, how to build shelter, how to preserve and prepare. Those skills were dangerous to the owning class because they represented residual independence. The factory system required, and produced, a different kind of human: one whose skills were specific to a single task on a production line, who depended entirely on wages, whose time was divided into productive and non-productive units, and who had no meaningful relationship to the thing they made or the land they stood on. This was not a side effect of industrial organization. It was its purpose.

The mechanism is visible in the food system alone. Subsistence farming — the direct production of food by people who ate what they grew — was the norm for most of human history. Industrialization created conditions in which subsistence farming became impossible for most people: land enclosed or too expensive, time consumed by wage work, skills allowed to atrophy across generations. Into the gap it created, industrialization inserted a food distribution industry that now claims to solve the problem of feeding people — the same problem it created by making self-sufficiency economically untenable. This is not irony. It is design logic. The machine creates the need it claims to fill.

The factory was the first machine. Finance became the second — more refined, because it could extract without requiring the worker to leave home.

What emerged was the dependency loop. The machine requires large populations to function — as labor, as consumers, as the mass that justifies its own scale. Large populations require the machine to survive — for food distribution, shelter, employment, healthcare. The loop is self-sealing. It is not a conspiracy in the sense of a boardroom of villains drafting blueprints. It is more sophisticated than that: a system that has made itself appear as necessary as oxygen.

A clarification the argument requires: the population observation is not misanthropy. The problem is not human numbers. The problem is the system that requires those numbers to maintain itself, and that produced the conditions — wage dependency, urbanization, the destruction of subsistence alternatives — that made that population growth both possible and structurally necessary. The argument is not that there are too many people. The argument is that the machine and the population it supports are co-constitutive. You cannot have one without the other, which is exactly how the machine wants it.

V. The Financialization of Being Human

If the enclosure of the commons was the first act and industrialization was the second, the third act is the one we are living through now: the financialization of everything that remained human. Creativity. Community. Attention. Identity. Meaning itself.

Art did not simply get commercialized. It got enclosed — in the same sense that land was enclosed. The mechanisms are different but the logic is identical: convert something held in common, outside the market, into something owned, distributed, and monetized by a centralized entity. The recording industry did not give artists an audience. It gave artists a landlord. The streaming platform did not democratize music — though it widened access in ways that are real and not nothing. A musician in 1985 needed a label to reach anyone. A musician in 2025 can upload to the world from a bedroom. That is a genuine change.

And the terms of that access are extractive in ways that are not incidental. Spotify pays between $0.003 and $0.005 per stream. A million streams — a number that represents genuine cultural reach, real human attention — returns a few thousand dollars. That is not a market. That is rent. The platform owns the distribution channel the way a landlord owns the building, and the artist, like the tenant, has the freedom to participate or not participate, with the understanding that not participating means not being heard. The appearance of choice within structurally constrained options is one of the system’s most refined products.

Community itself — the campfire, the commons, the choir — has been financialized. Housing is an investment vehicle. Neighborhoods are portfolios. The question of who lives near whom is determined not by relationship, shared labor, or mutual need, but by capital. A community that produces for itself, that meets its own needs through direct exchange and mutual aid, is a market failure — not metaphorically, but technically. It fails to generate the transactions the system requires. And market failures, in a system that has colonized the legal, political, and cultural mechanisms of society, tend to be corrected. Through zoning. Through taxation. Through debt. Through the steady elimination of any arrangement that does not require the machine.

No one needs to intend this. The system selects against self-sufficiency automatically. The abolition of conditions under which people do not need the market does not require villains. It requires incentives that reward the market’s expansion and penalize its circumvention. That is what five thousand years of refinement produces: a system that feels like nature because it has outlasted everyone who remembers the alternative.

VI. The Immune System and the Self-Organizing World

The human body maintains itself through a system that does not require a central authority. No neuron issues directives. No organ holds a vote. The immune system identifies disruption and responds — locally, proportionally, with information distributed across the whole. When a cell begins behaving in ways that threaten the organism, the organism responds. Not with courts. Not with politics. With biology.

The cosmos mirrors this at every scale. Ecosystems self-regulate. Mycelial networks distribute resources across vast distances with no central node, no hierarchy, no command structure — only relationship and response. Flocking birds produce complex, coherent, adaptive behavior with no leader and no plan. These are not primitive systems awaiting the sophistication of hierarchy. They are more sophisticated than hierarchy — more resilient, more adaptive, more capable of responding to conditions that no central planner could anticipate.

The immune system metaphor has limits that should be acknowledged. Immune systems do have centralized components — lymph nodes, the thymus, bone marrow. They do make catastrophic mistakes; autoimmune disorders are the body attacking itself. The metaphor is not a blueprint. It is a pointer toward a possibility: that complex, accountable, adaptive coordination can exist without top-down command. That the capacity for self-regulation is not something humans lack. It is something that has been systematically dismantled.

What we call government, law, and institutional authority are presented as solutions to the problem of human chaos — as if without them we would descend immediately into violence and disorder. The historical record suggests a more complicated picture: that hierarchy is itself a primary generator of systemic violence, and that the apparatus of state control creates conditions of dependency and alienation that it then claims to manage. The prison does not solve the poverty that produces crime. It requires it.

The anarchist tradition — from Kropotkin’s documentation of mutual aid as the primary driver of evolutionary survival, to contemporary theorists of prefigurative politics — has consistently argued that human beings are not naturally hierarchical animals who require authority to function. We are naturally cooperative animals who have been organized into hierarchies that serve the interests of those at the top.

The social Darwinism that became dominant in the late nineteenth century — competition as nature’s law, survival of the fittest as economic gospel — was not a scientific conclusion. It was a political project, using a distorted reading of Darwin to make exploitation look like biology. Kropotkin spent years observing species under stress and found the opposite: cooperation, not competition, was what allowed organisms and communities to survive. He was not refuting Darwin. He was correcting the theft of him.

The scale objection deserves a direct answer. A skeptic will say: at the scale of billions, courts and police and formal institutions are indispensable. You cannot coordinate eight billion people without hierarchy. That is probably true — at the scale of billions, organized by the systems that created and require that scale. But scale is not a fixed condition. Scale is a product. The industrial system produced the population density and dependency that make formal authority appear indispensable, and then points to that density as justification for the authority. The circularity is not incidental. It is the system’s most elegant self-defense.

VII. The Larval Stage: Adversity as Transformation

Plato’s cave: the shadows on the wall are taken for reality until something forces the prisoner to turn and see the light. The liberation is not comfortable. The light hurts. The other prisoners, still watching the shadows, think the escapee has gone mad.

Gnostic cosmology: the material world is a degraded emanation, dense and confining, from which consciousness seeks liberation — not because matter is evil but because it is incomplete, a stage rather than a destination.

Buddhist samsara: the cycle of conditioned existence, driven by ignorance and attachment, perpetuated by the refusal or inability to see what is actually happening. Awakening is not escape from the world. It is the end of being fooled by it.

These traditions do not agree on the mechanism. They agree on the diagnosis: that the world as it presents itself to unconsidered experience is not the whole story, and that the pressure of material existence — its suffering, its limitation, its adversarial nature — is not simply a problem to be solved. It is a process. The caterpillar does not escape the cocoon by reasoning its way out. It dissolves. It becomes, briefly, something that is neither what it was nor what it will be. The pressure is not incidental to the transformation. The pressure is the transformation.

I am not attempting to prove this cosmologically. I am offering it as a framework that makes sense of something the materialist account cannot fully explain: why the pressure of a system designed to produce compliance sometimes produces, instead, the exact minds that see through it. Punk was not supposed to happen. The underground was not supposed to persist. The question that wouldn’t die was not supposed to get answered.

A reasonable challenge: if pressure is transformation, why resist the machine? Isn’t the machine just more pressure, therefore more transformation? The distinction is this — natural adversity has a relationship to the organism it presses against. It is calibrated by response, by feedback, by the living exchange between resistance and capacity. Natural pressure meets the organism and adjusts. The machine meets resistance and applies more pressure. One is a conversation between a living thing and a world that will test it. The other is a crusher with no interest in what survives.

If material existence is a larval stage — a nursery for consciousness working toward something the nursery itself cannot contain — then the machine is not only an oppressive structure. It is also, incidentally, a whetstone. It does not intend to produce thinkers. It intends to produce workers. But the same pressure that is designed to keep consciousness small and compliant has, throughout history, generated the conditions of its own seeing.

This is the thesis the essay has been building toward, and this is where it lives most fully: the machine is a grotesque and unnecessary amplification of natural adversity. It takes what might be the productive friction of a life lived fully — the friction that builds, that teaches, that transforms — and converts it into the grinding dehumanization of manufactured need, wage dependency, and systematic alienation from the self. Natural adversity is the pressure that produces. The machine is the pressure that destroys. The difference is not in the force but in the purpose, and the purpose of the machine has never been transformation. It has been compliance.

The two registers — material and metaphysical, immune system and chrysalis — are not in competition. They are describing the same terrain at different altitudes. The machine can be named in the language of economics and history: debt, enclosure, wage dependency, financialization. But naming it does not explain why it matters, why the pressure of it sometimes produces not just exhaustion but seeing. That requires a different language. One that takes seriously the possibility that we are here to become something the machine cannot afford us to become.

VIII. The Sophistication of It

It is tempting to dismiss systemic critique as conspiracy thinking — the attribution of complex outcomes to a single coordinated malevolent intent. That dismissal is itself a function of the system’s sophistication. A conspiracy requires conscious coordination. What we are describing requires only incentive.

But the mechanism needs to be named, not just asserted. How does a system with no center make itself appear as the only possible arrangement of human life? Through institutions that reproduce themselves by reproducing the people inside them. Through education systems that present the current arrangement as the culmination of human progress. Through media that frames the boundaries of legitimate debate. Through the foreclosure of alternatives — no land left unowned, no skill left un-monetized, no time left unaccounted for, no commons left unenclosed. The cage is not built from a single blueprint. It is assembled, incrementally, from thousands of decisions made by people who were themselves inside a previous version of it.

The selection pressure argument requires historical evidence, not just assertion. Failed systems provide it. The commons — the shared land systems of pre-enclosure England, the integrated agricultural communities of East Asia, the guild structures of medieval European craft production — were not eliminated because they were inefficient. The commons were often more productive per acre than the enclosed private estates that replaced them. Guilds maintained quality and worker conditions that the factory system immediately abandoned. These systems were eliminated because they did not efficiently extract surplus upward. The factory beat the guild not because it produced better goods but because it produced more profit for fewer owners. The enclosure beat the commons not because it fed people better but because it fed landlords better. Extraction-efficient systems out-competed welfare-efficient systems because the people making the decisions were the people who extracted. This is not a conspiracy. This is what selection pressure looks like across centuries.

The organizational intelligence that encodes, reproduces, and defends a social order across millennia does not require a single designer any more than evolution requires one. It requires only that arrangements which concentrate power also tend to reproduce themselves — through the legal systems they write, the educational institutions they fund, the cultural narratives they elevate, and the alternatives they quietly eliminate. The system looks designed because it has been optimized. Optimization across sufficient time, under consistent selection pressure, produces the appearance of intent. That appearance is the system’s most powerful product.

“The world is not a conspiracy” is true and insufficient. The world is something more dangerous than a conspiracy: a set of self-reinforcing systems that produce outcomes as consistent as conspiracy, without requiring anyone to conspire. Conspiracy can be exposed and dismantled. Selection pressure cannot be dismantled by exposure. It can only be interrupted by the deliberate construction of alternative selection pressures — communities that reward cooperation over extraction, systems that value self-sufficiency over dependency, relationships that exist outside the logic of the market.

That is not ideology. That is engineering — the slow, deliberate construction of conditions under which the machine loses its hold. What that construction looks like, feels like, lives like, is the final question.

IX. What Punk Knew and Anarchism Completes

Punk was always an incomplete anarchism. It understood, viscerally and immediately, that the social contract was a fraud — that what was being sold as civilization was a set of arrangements designed to benefit those who designed them, maintained through the manufactured consent of those who did not. It understood that authenticity was not possible within systems that commodified everything, including rebellion. It understood that the correct response to a machine that wanted to turn you into a product was to be, loudly and sometimes badly, irreducibly yourself.

Punk had its own theory, written in three chords and the spaces between them. It knew that the system could not be reasoned with, only refused. It knew that the refusal had to be immediate, physical, and present — not deferred to a future revolution or a theoretical framework. What punk didn’t always have was the history: the knowledge that people had been refusing this same system, in different languages, for centuries. Anarchism gave me that history. Punk gave me the refusal that made me look for it.

The punk I mean is not a uniform or a genre. It is a refusal. Against Me!, The Gaslight Anthem, NOFX — these are not just bands. They are field notes from people who looked at the arrangement and said no, not because they had a theory, but because something in them recognized the shape of the cage. That recognition is not enough. But it is where the excavation begins.

What punk sometimes lacked was the theoretical architecture to sustain the instinct — to move from this is wrong to here is why, here is the history, here is what a different arrangement might look like. Anarchism provides that architecture. I am not offering anarchism as a finished project or a pure lineage. The movement has its own complicities, its own failures, its own moments of authoritarian drift dressed in anti-authoritarian language. I am not interested in defending everything done in its name. I am interested in the ideas that survived those failures, because they are the ones that match what the historical record shows and what the body already knows.

Kropotkin: mutual aid is not idealism. It is the historical record. Human beings built everything that matters — language, culture, agriculture, medicine, art — through cooperation, not competition. The narrative of the heroic individual accumulator is a story told by accumulators.

Proudhon: property — in the specific sense of the right to extract rent from what you own but do not use — is not a natural right. It is a legal construction that converts the commons into private extraction. When you pay rent, you are not compensating someone for shelter. You are paying tribute to someone who was granted, by a legal system written by property owners, the right to deny you access to the earth unless you pay.

Graeber: debt is not a neutral financial instrument. It is a moral weapon — the oldest mechanism for converting free people into dependents, for making obligation feel like character, for making the refusal to submit feel like theft. The moralization of debt — the insistence that to owe is to be diminished, that the indebted person is a lesser person — is not accidental. It is load-bearing.

Goldman: a revolution that does not make room for joy, creativity, and the full expression of human being is not worth having. The campfire is not a luxury. The choir is not a reward for after the work is done. They are the point. They are what we are.

X. The Codex

The child who wanted to find the hidden temple was not wrong. The temple exists. The codex is real. It is written across 5,000 years of clay tablets, enclosure acts, labor records, colonial charters, patent filings, and mortgage agreements. It is not hidden. It is hiding in plain sight, which is a more sophisticated concealment — the lie told so thoroughly, from so many directions, for so long, that most people experience its walls as the horizon.

The load-bearing lie, restated plainly: that the arrangements of modern life — wage labor, rent, debt, the state’s monopoly on legitimate violence, the conversion of commons into private property — are natural, inevitable, and the best available expression of human nature. They are none of these things. They are specific, historical, documented constructions, built to serve specific interests, maintained through specific mechanisms, experienced as nature only by those who have never been shown the seams.

Seeing the seams does not dissolve the machine. It does something more important: it ends the machine’s claim on your imagination. You cannot be epistemically caged by a story you can see through. You cannot be made to feel that your inability to thrive inside a system designed to extract from you is a personal failure, once you understand what the system is designed to do.

A reasonable objection: if the system were that sophisticated, you wouldn’t be able to write this. The fact that this essay exists seems to disprove the totality of the control. The objection is correct. The system is not total. It never has been. The underground persisted. The subculture survived. The question that wouldn’t die got answered, not because the machine failed to try, but because the thing it was trying to suppress is not a belief or an idea that can be suppressed. It is a capacity — the capacity for humans to recognize, under sufficient pressure, the shape of what is pressing on them. The machine produces its own resistance the way a grinding stone produces sparks. It does not intend to. It cannot help it.

That is the paradox the essay has been working toward: the system is powerful but not total. It produces the conditions of its own seeing. The pressure it applies — the adversity it deploys — is the same pressure that, in sufficient concentration, dissolves the story the pressure was designed to maintain.

I have spent thirty-five years looking for the temple. I found it. The artifact inside is not gold. It is the knowledge that the machine is not total, that the seams are visible, that the imagination it tried to colonize still belongs to me.

That is the internal win. It matters.

But the machine does not care about internal wins. It cares about participation. And participation is the one thing it cannot force — only make feel necessary.

So the question I am left with, the one I cannot answer for you, is this: what are you participating in that you do not need?

Not everything. Not all at once. One thing. An hour. A conversation. A refusal.

The campfire is not a metaphor. It is what people do when they stop performing for the machine and start being present to each other. It is what we have always done. It is what the machine has always tried to make unnecessary, inconvenient, impractical, unprofitable.

It failed. It keeps failing. We keep building fires.
