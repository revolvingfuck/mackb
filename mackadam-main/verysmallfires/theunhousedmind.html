The Unhoused Mind

Three years of making and moving without a home

Essay • Housing & Creativity • 1,252 words

I’ve been making things for three years without a proper place to live. Games, stories, music, strange little experiments with AI. All of it pulled out of thin air while sleeping on couches, dragging a duffel bag across Vancouver, hoping the next week would be less temporary than the last.

It doesn’t add up. I kept producing, but I couldn’t keep a roof. That contradiction ate at me. Was it just me screwing up, or was there something bigger going on—something about this city, this country, and the way we treat the people who try to build culture here?

The numbers say I’m not imagining it. Canada’s creative industries pump billions into the economy every year, but the people doing the work—the ones painting, coding, composing—live closer to eviction than stability. The city loves to brag about its “creative economy” while the workers it depends on are priced out of every block.

In Vancouver, apartments are illegal on three-quarters of the land. Think about that: a city in crisis, thousands short of housing, but most of its dirt locked up for detached homes and speculation. The result is simple: to live here, you either inherit property or you bleed yourself dry. Everyone else crashes on couches, hustles rent money, or leaves.

I’ve walked those streets with nothing but a backpack while condos stood empty—glass towers glowing like trophies nobody could afford. I know musicians who tour Europe but can’t scrape together first and last month’s rent. Game developers who sell a hit on Steam and still move back in with their parents. The economy eats what they make but spits them out.

There are nights that stay with me. One winter I crashed on a friend’s sofa in East Van, my bag tucked behind the door so it wouldn’t be in the way. At 3 a.m., I watched snow fall through a cracked window, and all I could think about was how many people in this city were watching the same storm from tents and cars. How many of them were making things too—poems, paintings, beats on busted laptops—and how little that counted when the rent was due.

It isn’t just Vancouver. Vienna builds housing like it’s a public utility—tens of thousands of new units, backed by land banks older than the Second World War. Tokyo tore up its zoning laws and added over a million homes in a decade. Singapore turned housing into a system of long leases so regular people could own a place without being devoured by speculators. Meanwhile, we pat ourselves on the back for approving a few mid-rises after years of debate.

I think about what that means for artists here. In Vienna, half the city lives in social housing, and their orchestras, galleries, and theatres still thrive. In Tokyo, young creators can rent a small place, find a studio, survive long enough to finish their projects. Here, we spend more energy chasing the next sublet than finishing the work itself. Creativity gets reduced to a survival skill, a way of improvising when the system offers nothing stable.

And when creative workers try to find support elsewhere—grants, funding, platforms—the game doesn’t change. Arts councils drown you in forms designed for people with office jobs. Patreon rewards the kind of art that looks good in a feed, not the kind that takes years to bloom. The platforms that profit off our work—Spotify, YouTube—strip us down to fractions of pennies. You can pour your soul into a song or a book, and the algorithm won’t even notice.

I once spent weeks applying for a grant, only to be told I’d missed a formatting requirement on a budget page. Meanwhile, the same council funds institutions that pay artists less than minimum wage to perform. The gatekeepers get paid; the creators get exposure. If you’ve ever sat in a rehearsal space you couldn’t afford, working for an honorarium that barely covers bus fare, you know the humiliation of being told you should be grateful for crumbs.

We like to tell ourselves that Canada values artists, that we’re a country of culture as much as resources. But if that were true, the people making the work wouldn’t be living one eviction notice away from disappearing. We’d have rehearsal spaces instead of Airbnbs, tax breaks for people building worlds instead of condos, real support instead of gatekeeping.

The truth is harder: the system isn’t broken. It’s working exactly as designed. Homeowners vote, developers profit, artists scatter. The rest of us are left with stories about how “vibrant” the city used to be.

So what do we do with that? Pretend? Or admit that the creative class in Canada has been set up to fail, that the same hands we trust to make meaning out of this mess are the ones least able to survive it?

I don’t want to write policy recommendations. I’m not a think tank. I’m someone who’s been homeless and still trying to make something that matters. I don’t have a framework. I just have the ache of what it feels like to create in a city that doesn’t care if you vanish tomorrow.

Maybe the point isn’t to fix it with more bureaucracy. Maybe the point is to name it, to say out loud that a country that starves its artists is already bankrupt in ways no GDP figure can hide. That a city without room for imagination is already dead, no matter how tall its towers climb.

I’ve kept working anyway. Because that’s what artists do—we make in spite of, not because of. Maybe the wars we inherit aren’t fought with rifles and mud, but with eviction notices and side hustles. Maybe the point isn’t winning but refusing to quit, even when the math says you should have given up years ago.

And here’s the part that still surprises me: there’s beauty in that refusal. In writing a story at 2 a.m. on borrowed Wi-Fi. In recording vocals in a basement that smells like mildew. In sketching out levels for a game while the laundromat machines rattle around you. Creation isn’t just survival—it’s defiance. Every finished piece is a middle finger to the system that wanted you invisible.

Tomorrow I’ll wake up, still without a permanent place, and try again to build something out of nothing. Maybe that’s all we get. Maybe that’s enough. Or maybe it’s more than enough—the proof that even in the cracks of a collapsing system, something alive can still grow.
